# Frequency-adaptive continuous fields for TorchSky

Status: experimental research note, August 2026.

## Motivation

The current TorchSky field stack already includes Fourier MLPs, SIREN/WIRE-like
models, multiresolution hash grids, Gaussian bases, spectral Power Foam, and
representation-independent detector-pixel quadrature. The remaining scientific
problem is not simply adding more frequency capacity. For native astronomical
imaging, the useful representation should simultaneously:

- retain thin and faint local morphology;
- avoid fitting high-frequency noise over smooth low-surface-brightness regions;
- behave predictably when evaluated at a different spatial scale;
- compose with WCS, bandpass, PSF, detector, background, and uncertainty operators;
- remain tensor-native and scalable across CPU, CUDA, and MPS.

This note selects two additional INR families for immediate experimentation and
keeps anisotropic 2D Gaussian splatting as the next distinct representation track.

## 1. SASNet-inspired spatially adaptive frequency field

Feng et al., *SASNet: Spatially-Adaptive Sinusoidal Networks for INRs*, CVPR 2026,
combine explicit frozen frequency support with learned spatial masks generated by
a lightweight multiscale hash-grid MLP. Their goal is directly relevant here:
prevent high-frequency leakage into smooth regions while avoiding oversmoothing
where fine structure is genuinely required.

`SASNetField` adapts that principle to TorchSky's continuous
`(ra, dec, wavelength) -> surface brightness` interface. A frozen sinusoidal bank
spans the full field coordinates, while an existing TorchSky `HashGridEncoder`
over sky position predicts per-frequency masks. This keeps the implementation
pure PyTorch and reuses TorchSky's WCS/operator/rendering substrate.

### Why this is the highest-priority new field

Astronomical scenes mix smooth backgrounds, diffuse emission, unresolved sources,
and thin elongated structures. A single global frequency allocation makes the
fine-detail/noise tradeoff unnecessarily global. Spatial frequency masks give a
mechanism for allocating spectral capacity only where the data support it.

### Main risks

- The mask network may itself memorize noise if its finest hash-grid level is too
  fine or insufficiently regularized.
- Spatial masks do not solve the wavelength null space of broadband observations.
- A masked sinusoid remains globally supported before masking, so benchmarking
  localization and extrapolation is essential.

## 2. BACON-inspired band-limited multiscale field

Lindell et al., *BACON: Band-Limited Coordinate Networks for Multiscale Scene
Representation*, CVPR 2022, use multiplicative sinusoidal filters with controlled
spectral support and intermediate outputs representing progressively finer
scales. The important property for TorchSky is predictable scale behavior rather
than raw reconstruction capacity.

`BaconField` implements a compact TorchSky adaptation with frozen incremental
sinusoidal filters, multiplicative hidden states, and a readout at every cumulative
bandwidth. It is intended as a controlled multiscale baseline for native-image
reconstruction and anti-aliasing studies.

### Why include BACON when newer sinusoidal INRs exist

TUNER (Novello et al., CVPR 2025) provides stronger modern theory for sinusoidal
frequency initialization and bounds the generated network spectrum during
training. It is an attractive follow-up. A faithful TUNER implementation,
however, requires enforcing parameter constraints after optimizer steps. TorchSky
should add that through a generic post-step projection/constraint hook rather than
silently embedding optimizer-specific behavior in one model. BACON therefore has
a cleaner model-only integration today and supplies an interpretable multiscale
baseline.

### Main risks

- Hard spectral control can produce ringing near sharp boundaries.
- The appropriate normalized-coordinate bandwidth must be related to the actual
  WCS/pixel/PSF scale; arbitrary bandwidth tuning would defeat the purpose.
- BACON controls representable scale but does not localize capacity spatially as
  SASNet does.

## Why mip-NeRF/IPE is not a separate model priority

Mip-NeRF's integrated positional encoding addresses finite observation footprints
and scale-dependent aliasing. TorchSky already provides representation-independent
2x2 Gauss-Legendre spatial quadrature for field rendering, so every field model
can be trained against an approximation to the detector-pixel integral rather
than only pixel centers. That operator-level solution should be benchmarked first.

Integrated coordinate features could later reduce the number of field evaluations
needed for multiscale rendering, but they are not a replacement for the physical
pixel/PSF forward operator and are therefore not one of the first new field types.

## Why 2D Gaussian splatting remains the next representation track

GaussianImage (ECCV 2024) demonstrates fast compact 2D anisotropic Gaussian image
representation, and GaussianImage++ (AAAI 2026) improves allocation with
content-dependent densification. An astronomy-native variant is particularly
appealing because anisotropic Gaussians naturally span compact sources, elongated
arc segments, and broad diffuse structure, while Gaussian PSF convolution has
useful analytic structure.

The existing TorchSky `GaussianBasisField` is not yet this representation: it is
a small dense 3D Gaussian basis over normalized `(x, y, wavelength)` and evaluates
all basis functions at all requested coordinates. A survey-scale splat field
should instead have anisotropic 2D covariances, continuous spectral coefficients,
spatial indexing/tiled candidate lists, and adaptive primitive management.

Vanilla image-compression densification is not scientifically appropriate for
low-surface-brightness astronomy. GaussianImage++ allocates primitives according
to image distortion/signal content; an astronomy implementation should instead
be driven by uncertainty-normalized, cross-exposure-persistent residual evidence.
Otherwise stars and bright compact sources can consume capacity while faint arcs
and diffuse emission remain underrepresented.

For those reasons spectral 2D Gaussian splatting is a high-priority design track,
but should not be added as a naive `O(N_pixels * N_gaussians)` production field.

## Benchmark gate

The new fields should be compared with the existing Fourier MLP, WIRE, recommended
feature/hash-grid fields, and spectral Power Foam under the *same observation
operator, optimizer budget, and parameter-count envelope*.

The primary science gate should use controlled native-exposure injections across
subpixel phases and surface-brightness levels. Report at minimum:

1. uncertainty-weighted native measurement loss on retained data;
2. peak and aperture-response curves;
3. morphology correlation and centerline recall for thin arcs;
4. support IoU/recall only as stress-test diagnostics, not completeness estimates;
5. dither-to-dither consistency before and after pixel-footprint quadrature;
6. spatial-frequency power/coherence against injected truth on simulations;
7. wall time, peak memory, and scaling with number of queried samples.

A representation should not be promoted because it produces a sharper-looking
rendering. Promotion requires better recovery of known injected structure at
comparable held-measurement likelihood without increasing unsupported
high-frequency power.

## Recommended order

1. **SASNetField** — highest scientific prior for local faint morphology versus
   smooth/noisy background.
2. **BaconField** — controlled multiscale/band-limit baseline and diagnostic.
3. **Spectral anisotropic 2D Gaussian splatting** — design next, with sparse
   rendering and noise-aware, cross-exposure-persistent densification.
4. **TUNER** — add after a general train-time parameter-projection hook exists.

WIRE, spectral Power Foam, multiresolution hash grids, and detector-pixel
quadrature already exist in TorchSky and should be baselines rather than new
implementations.
